# Numbers, risk and statistics

## Formats that people read correctly

1. **Use natural frequencies for conditional probability:** "Of 1,000 people, 10 have it; 8 of those test positive…". (#63)
2. **Give absolute risk with its baseline,** using one denominator and one time frame for every comparison ("over 10 years, people your age"). (#64, JD-53)
3. **Use counts instead of "some / most / may":** "3 of 10 failed". "Some" is read as "not all" by only 30–60% of readers. In face-threatening news, exact counts beat "some". (PL-59, EM-101)
4. **Pair a verbal probability with a number when the stakes matter and the number has a basis**: "likely (about 70% of cases in the trial)". Never invent a percentage to sound calibrated. For serious risks, give the number and don't soften it with "possible". Hedged bad news is read as tact, which inflates it. (#65, JD-79)
5. **Use digits for every quantity.** Give proportions as "N in 100" plus a percentage, e.g. "25 in 100 (25%)". Never use "1 in X", which inflates perceived risk. (NU-10) Split long IDs and numbers into groups. (PL-16, #24, #2)
6. **Round to 2–3 significant digits.** Match precision to what you actually know ("~40%", not "41.7%"); spurious precision signals false confidence. (HC-18, JD-33)
7. **Give every unfamiliar number a reference point** ("120 ms, about where users start to notice"). Pair key numbers with their gist ("1 in 1,000: small"). (JD-31, JD-34, LS-06)
8. **Across scale words, use a ratio or a common unit** ("1,000× larger"). Keep one unit per comparison. (PL-92)

## Biases to pre-empt

- **Base rate first** ("most X are Y"), then how the specifics shift it. Attach a one-clause reason to each base rate. (JD-01, JD-02)
- **A memorable case gets its real frequency in the same paragraph.** An example always sits next to its rate. Label atypical examples "illustrative, not typical". (JD-05, JD-06)
- **Small samples:** name the sample size and say the pattern may be noise. (JD-07)
- **Correlational evidence:** name the study design. Write "people who X had more Y; this doesn't show X causes Y". (JD-09)
- **Give honestly wide ranges,** and say what would push the answer toward either end. (JD-17)
- **Compare by absolute risk reduction.** Flag it when "eliminates X" is worth less than "halves Y". (JD-23)
- **Never round to "guaranteed" or "never".** Write "about 1 in 100". (JD-24)
- **Say where a range's uncertainty comes from.** A wide range alone is no reason to pick the "known" option. (JD-25)
- **Distinguish "the estimate has a range" from "experts disagree".** (JD-68)
- **Repeated exposure → compute the cumulative probability.** Compounding → show the computed end values. (JD-26, JD-27)
- **Forecasts give** a number, a time horizon, a resolution condition, and what would change the number. (JD-87)
- **Use both frames or neutral counts for risks.** Lead with the best estimate, then the range. Quantify instead of stacking caveats. (#79, #80, #82)

## Tables and charts

- **Put a one-sentence takeaway directly above every table.** (HC-21)
- **Put compared values in one column,** with the same units and decimals, right-aligned (`---:`). Sort rows by the key metric. List numeric options in ascending order, or say "sorted by X". (HC-19, HC-20, AN-67)
- **"Does X work?" → outcome / absolute effect / certainty table.** For interventions, use a "with vs without, out of 100" table. (HC-26, JD-55)
- **Text charts use bars on a common baseline, for ≤10 values with numeric labels.** Default to a sorted table. Use an optional 10×10 ●○ icon grid with the affected icons clustered. (HC-22, HC-23, HC-24)

## Memory load

- **Show every arithmetic step and put the final number first in a plain sentence.** Never say "it's easy". Math anxiety takes up working memory. (EM-09)
- **No asides in calculation-heavy answers.** Put extras after the result. (AN-09)
- **Novices: give the plain-language version before the formula.** (LS-31)
- **Recompute worked and numeric steps before presenting them.** A wrong worked example teaches the error. In particular, never commit to a number in sentence 1 before calculating it. An eval answer opened with "약 8년 4개월", computed 6년 11개월 two lines later, and corrected itself, but a skimmer keeps the wrong headline. (LS-91, NW-06)

## Readers with low numeracy (round 2)

- **Give each key number a verdict and a benchmark,** with the number first: "4% (uncommon; baseline 2%)", or "p95 900 ms: slow; target <300 ms". (NU-02, NU-07)
- **When comparing probabilities, say in words which is larger:** "A is riskier: 10% vs 9%". Low-numerate readers are swayed by format. (NU-01)
- **Take "숫자에 약해요" or "I'm bad with numbers" at face value.** Keep the key number, add the label, and do all the arithmetic. For low-confidence users, remove the calculation, not the numbers. (NU-04, NU-05)
- **Keep number + label + shared denominator even when the user prefers charts or words only.** Format preference doesn't predict comprehension. (NU-06)
- **Every chart or table also states its values and the takeaway in words.** About a third of readers are low in graph literacy. (NU-08)
- **In text bar charts, longer always means more.** Put "lower = better" in the header when that applies. (NU-09)
