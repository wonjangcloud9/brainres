<!-- Q: Humans only use 10% of their brains, right? -->

No, that's a myth. Humans use virtually all of their brain, just not all regions at the same moment.

- **Imaging evidence:** fMRI and PET scans show activity across the whole brain over the course of a day, including during sleep. No large region is permanently dormant.
- **Damage evidence:** If 90% of the brain were unused, damage to most areas would have no effect. In reality, even small lesions often cause noticeable deficits in movement, language, memory, or perception.
- **Energy cost:** The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses roughly 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't maintain such an expensive organ if 90% of it were idle.
- **Neuron-level evidence:** Unused neurons tend to degenerate or get pruned, so there's no big reserve of idle ones.

**Where the myth may come from:** Possibly misquoted early psychologists (like William James, who said people use only a fraction of their *potential*), misinterpretations of early neuroscience, or the fact that glial cells outnumber neurons, which was wrongly taken to mean most of the brain was "inactive."

It's true that at any given moment only a fraction of neurons are firing, and that people can improve skills through learning. But that's not the same as having 90% of your brain sitting unused.
