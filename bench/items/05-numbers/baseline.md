<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

**About 9%**, assuming "99% accurate" means the test has 99% sensitivity (catches 99% of true cases) and 99% specificity (correctly clears 99% of healthy people).

Take 100,000 people:

- **Have the disease:** 100 (1 in 1,000)
  - Test positive: 99
- **Don't have it:** 99,900
  - False positives (1%): about 999

Total positives ≈ 99 + 999 = 1,098. Of those, 99 are real.

99 / 1,098 ≈ **9%**

The disease is so rare that the 1% false-positive rate applied to the huge healthy group produces roughly 10 times more false positives than there are true cases.

Practical takeaways:

- A positive result is a reason for a confirmatory test, not a diagnosis.
- The answer depends on your prior risk. If you have symptoms or risk factors that put you in a higher-risk group than 1 in 1,000, the probability goes up substantially.
- If "99% accurate" means something different for this specific test (e.g., different sensitivity and specificity), the number shifts. Your doctor can tell you the actual figures.
