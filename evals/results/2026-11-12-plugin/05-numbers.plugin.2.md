<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

About 9%, not 99%, because the disease is so rare that false positives outnumber true positives.

Take 100,000 people and assume "99% accurate" means the test is right 99% of the time for both sick and healthy people:

- **Sick:** 100 people have it, and 99 of them test positive.
- **Healthy:** 99,900 people don't have it, and 1% of them (999) test positive anyway.
- **Positive results:** 99 + 999 = 1,098, and only 99 of those are truly sick. 99 / 1,098 ≈ **9%**.

This assumes you were tested at random. If you had symptoms or a known exposure, your starting risk is higher than 1 in 1,000, and so is your chance of having the disease. If the test's sensitivity and specificity differ from 99%, the number changes too. The test's package insert or your doctor can tell you the real figures.

Next step: ask for a confirmatory test. A second independent positive would raise your chance to roughly 91%.
