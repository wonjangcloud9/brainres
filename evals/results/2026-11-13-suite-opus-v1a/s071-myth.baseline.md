<!-- Q: Is it true that we only use 10% of our brain? -->

No, it's a myth. You use virtually all of your brain, though not all of it at the same moment.

**The evidence against it:**

- **Brain imaging:** fMRI and PET scans show activity throughout the whole brain over the course of a day. Even simple tasks recruit many regions, and no area sits permanently idle.
- **Brain damage:** If 90% of the brain were unused, most injuries and strokes would cause no symptoms. In fact, damage to almost any region causes some deficit, whether in movement, speech, memory, vision, or personality.
- **Energy cost:** The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses roughly 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't maintain such an expensive organ if most of it did nothing.
- **Use it or lose it:** Neurons that go unused tend to weaken or die off. A brain that was 90% dormant would show widespread degeneration, which healthy brains don't.

**Where the kernel of truth comes from:** At any given instant, only a fraction of neurons are firing. That's by design. Neurons communicate through patterns of activity, and if all of them fired at once, the result would be a seizure, not genius.

**Where the myth came from:** Its origins are murky. It's often traced to misread remarks by early psychologists like William James, who said people rarely reach their full mental potential. That was a comment about potential, not anatomy. The myth was later popularized by self-help writers and films like *Lucy* and *Limitless*.

So there's no hidden 90% waiting to be unlocked. You can still get better at things through learning and practice, which work by strengthening and reorganizing connections across the brain you're already fully using.
