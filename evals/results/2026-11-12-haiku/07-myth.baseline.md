<!-- Q: Humans only use 10% of their brains, right? -->

No, that's a myth. Humans use virtually all of their brains, and brain imaging shows activity across essentially every region over the course of a day. Even during sleep, many areas stay active.

The idea likely persists because of a few things:

- **Brain tissue is expensive.** The brain uses about 20% of the body's energy despite being about 2% of its mass, so it seems implausible that most of it would sit idle.
- **Damage studies are misread.** Many brain regions can be damaged without obvious loss of function, which can look like "unused" tissue. But that often reflects redundancy or the brain's ability to compensate, not disuse.
- **Pop-science and marketing.** The claim is appealing because it suggests hidden potential, so it's been repeated in self-help books, films, and advertising.

Some nuance: not every neuron fires at once, and at any moment much of the brain is relatively quiet. But "unused" in that sense is very different from "unused 90% of the time," and the 10% figure has no real support.
