<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

**About 9%.** That assumes "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of sick people and clears 99% of healthy people.

The result is low because the disease is rare, so false positives outnumber true ones. Picture 100,000 people:

| Group | People | Test positive |
|---|---|---|
| Have the disease (1 in 1,000) | 100 | 99 (99% caught) |
| Don't have it | 99,900 | 999 (1% false alarm) |
| **Total positive** | | **1,098** |

Only 99 of those 1,098 positives are real, so 99 ÷ 1,098 ≈ **9.0%**.

Two things can push your real number higher:

1. **Symptoms or risk factors.** The 1-in-1,000 rate applies to random people. If you were tested because of symptoms or exposure, your starting odds are higher, and so is your result.
2. **A second positive test.** If an independent second test is also positive, your odds rise to about 91% (9% prior → 99 true vs. about 10 false per positive pair).

Next step: ask your doctor for a confirmatory test, ideally a different type of test, before acting on this result.
