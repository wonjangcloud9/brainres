<!-- Q: Is it true that we only use 10% of our brain? -->

No, that's a myth: we use virtually all of our brain, just not all of it at the same moment.

The evidence against it:

- **Imaging:** fMRI and PET scans show activity across the whole brain over a day, including during sleep.
- **Damage:** There is no "spare" 90%. Damage to almost any region, even a small one, causes some deficit (stroke in a small area can impair speech, vision, or movement).
- **Energy cost:** The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy. Evolution would not maintain a costly organ that is 90% idle.
- **Anatomy:** Mapping finds a function for every major region, and neurons that go unused tend to die off.

At any single moment, only a fraction of neurons fire. That is by design: if all fired at once, you'd have something like a seizure. This may be one source of the myth.

The myth's origin is unclear. It is often traced to early 20th-century self-help writing, which misquoted William James's remark that people don't reach their full mental potential.
