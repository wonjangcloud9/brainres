<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

**About 9%.**

This assumes "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of people who have the disease (sensitivity) and correctly clears 99% of people who don't (specificity).

Imagine 100,000 people tested:

- **100** have the disease (1 in 1,000), and the test catches **99** of them.
- **99,900** don't have it, and 1% of them (**999**) get a false positive.

That's 99 + 999 = 1,098 positive results, and only 99 of them are real:

99 / 1,098 ≈ **9%**

The disease is rare, so the healthy people who test positive by mistake far outnumber the sick people who test positive correctly, even with a 1% error rate.

A positive result raises your odds from 0.1% to about 9%, which is roughly 90 times higher, but it's still far more likely than not that you're fine. The usual next step is a confirmatory test. Since the false positives are mostly random errors, a second positive would raise the probability a lot (to roughly 90% if the tests are independent).

If your doctor has given you the test's actual sensitivity and specificity, I can redo the calculation with those numbers. Also, if you have symptoms or risk factors, you're not a "random" person from the population, so your real odds would be higher than 9%.
