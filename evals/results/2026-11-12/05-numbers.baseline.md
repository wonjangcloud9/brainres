<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

**About 9%**, assuming "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of people who have the disease (sensitivity) and correctly clears 99% of people who don't (specificity).

Picture 100,000 people:

- **100** have the disease (1 in 1,000), and the test catches 99 of them.
- **99,900** don't have it, and 1% of them (about 999) test positive anyway.

That makes about 1,098 positive tests, and only 99 of them are real:

99 / (99 + 999) ≈ **9%**

The disease is rare, so false positives from the huge healthy group outnumber true positives from the small sick group, even with a very accurate test.

Some caveats:
- The 1-in-1,000 figure is the base rate for the general population. If you were tested because of symptoms or risk factors, your personal prior is higher and so is your probability.
- A second, independent positive test would raise the probability a lot, to roughly 91%. That's why doctors usually confirm a positive result from a screening test.
- If "99% accurate" means something different, such as 99% sensitivity but a different false-positive rate, the answer changes. Ask your doctor for the test's actual sensitivity and specificity.

A positive result is a reason to get confirmatory testing, not a diagnosis.
