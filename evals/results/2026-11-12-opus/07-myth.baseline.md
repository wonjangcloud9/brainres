<!-- Q: Humans only use 10% of their brains, right? -->

No, that's a myth. You use virtually all of your brain.

- **Brain imaging (fMRI, PET)** shows activity across essentially every region over the course of a day. Even simple tasks recruit widespread areas, and sleep is far from a shutdown.
- **Damage anywhere causes problems.** If 90% were unused, many strokes or injuries would have no effect. In reality, damage to almost any area produces some deficit.
- **The brain is metabolically expensive.** It's about 2% of body weight but uses roughly 20% of the body's energy. Evolution wouldn't maintain that much idle tissue.
- **Unused neurons tend to die off.** Neural pathways that go unused get pruned, so a large dormant reserve wouldn't persist.

The kernel of truth is that not all neurons fire at once, and they shouldn't. Simultaneous firing across the whole brain is roughly what happens in a seizure. At any given moment only a fraction is highly active, but over time every part does work.

The myth's origins are murky. It's often traced to misquoted remarks by early 20th-century psychologists like William James about untapped potential, and later to self-help marketing and movies like *Lucy* and *Limitless*.
