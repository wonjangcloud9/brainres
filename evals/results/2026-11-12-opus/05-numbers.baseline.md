<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

# About 9%, not 99%

Assuming "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of people who have the disease and correctly clears 99% of people who don't, here's what happens in a group of 100,000 people:

| | Test positive | Test negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Have the disease** (100) | 99 | 1 |
| **Don't have it** (99,900) | **999** (false positives) | 98,901 |

Of the **1,098** people who test positive, only **99** actually have the disease:

**99 ÷ 1,098 ≈ 9%**

## Why it's so low

The disease is rare. A 1% false positive rate applied to the large healthy population produces about 10 times more false alarms than there are real cases. This is called the **base rate fallacy**.

## Things that change the number

- **A second test helps a lot.** If an independent retest also comes back positive, the probability rises to about **91%**. That's why doctors usually confirm positive screening results.
- **Your personal risk matters.** "1 in 1,000" is the rate for the general population. If you were tested because you had symptoms, exposure, or family history, your starting odds are higher, and so is the chance the positive is real.
- **The test's real numbers matter.** "99% accurate" is vague. Real tests report sensitivity and specificity separately, and they're often different. A 99.9% specificity, for example, would raise your probability to about 50%.

If this is about a real result, ask your doctor about a confirmatory test and what the test's false positive rate is.
