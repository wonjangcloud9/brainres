<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

About 9%, assuming "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of real cases and wrongly flags 1% of healthy people.

**Worked example with 100,000 people**

| Group | People | Test positive |
|---|---|---|
| Have the disease (1 in 1,000) | 100 | 99 (true positives) |
| Don't have it | 99,900 | 999 (false positives, 1%) |
| **Total positives** | | **1,098** |

Only 99 of the 1,098 positives are real, so 99 ÷ 1,098 ≈ **9.0%**.

**Why it's so low:** The disease is rare, so healthy people outnumber sick people 999 to 1. A 1% error rate on that huge healthy group produces about 10 false alarms for every true case.

**What could change this estimate:**
- **Symptoms or risk factors:** If you were tested because of these, your starting odds are higher than 1 in 1,000, so your real chance is higher than 9%.
- **The test's actual figures:** Sensitivity (the rate of catching real cases) and specificity (the rate of correctly clearing healthy people) are often different numbers. Ask for both, because specificity matters most here.
- **A second test:** A second, independent positive raises the odds to about 91%, because the starting point is now 9% instead of 0.1%.

Next step: ask your doctor about a confirmatory test and what your personal risk was before testing.
