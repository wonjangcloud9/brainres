<!-- Q: A test for a disease is 99% accurate and 1 in 1,000 people have it. I tested positive. How likely is it that I actually have it? -->

About 9%, not 99%, assuming "99% accurate" means the test catches 99% of sick people and clears 99% of healthy ones. Check this assumption against your test's stated sensitivity and specificity.

Take 100,000 people:

- 100 have the disease. The test flags 99 of them (true positives).
- 99,900 are healthy. The test wrongly flags 1% of them, which is 999 (false positives).

So 99 + 999 = 1,098 people test positive, and only 99 of them are sick: 99 / 1,098 ≈ **9%**.

The reason is that the disease is rare. Healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones, so even a 1% false-positive rate produces roughly ten times more false alarms than real cases.

Your own odds could differ from 9% if you had symptoms or risk factors, because then you are not a random member of the 1-in-1,000 population. A doctor would also typically order a second, different test. A second positive would raise the probability to roughly 90%.

Next step: ask your doctor for a confirmatory test and for the exact sensitivity and specificity of the one you took.
